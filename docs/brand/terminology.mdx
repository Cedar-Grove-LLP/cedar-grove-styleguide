# Terminology

This is a **recommended starting glossary**, not a settled dictionary. Cedar Grove doesn't yet
have a large body of existing copy to reverse-engineer conventions from — the live site is
essentially a tagline, a nav shell, and a logo — so most of the calls below are reasoned
proposals grounded in the two real things this system does have to go on: the site's one
public line of copy ("We help good people build good organizations") and the vocabulary
already live in production inside the firm's own attorney-utilization and billing dashboard.
Where a row here doesn't match how people at the firm actually talk about their own work,
their usage should win — please correct it.

That's also the right way to think about this page over time. As Alla Kholmatova argues in
*Design Systems*, a shared vocabulary only stays useful if someone keeps curating it as new
terms show up — a glossary that's written once and never revisited quietly stops matching how
the team actually talks, and inconsistent usage creeps back in one document at a time. Treat
this table as living: when a new domain term comes up in real client work, it belongs here,
reviewed the same way a change to a token or component would be — see **Governance** in the
sidebar for how that review works.

## Preferred terms

| Use | Not | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Client** | Customer | Cedar Grove's relationships are professional and advisory — legal representation or organizational consulting — not retail transactions. "Customer" implies a purchase; "client" implies a relationship of trust and judgment, which is the actual nature of the work. |
| **Matter** | Case, Project | "Matter" is the standard legal-industry term for a discrete piece of client work, and it's already the term Cedar Grove's own analytics app uses internally — this recommendation follows real, active usage rather than introducing a new word. "Case" specifically implies litigation, which undersells or misdescribes the firm's non-litigation and consulting work. "Project" swings too far the other way — it's accurate in a generic sense but strips out the legal weight and formality the word "matter" carries. |
| **Attorney** | Lawyer | Both words are correct English and neither is a factual error — this is a pure consistency call, not an accuracy one. The recommendation is "attorney" specifically because it's the term already in use inside the firm's own production app (attorney utilization, not lawyer utilization). If people who actually practice at the firm prefer "lawyer" day to day, that's a real, legitimate reason to flip this recommendation — the point is picking one and using it everywhere, not this particular one being sacred. |
| **Sign in** | Log in | "Sign in" is the more current, plain-language convention across contemporary products, and it reads slightly less technical than "log in," which has a faint command-line/terminal connotation left over from an earlier era of software. |
| **Firm** | Company | Cedar Grove LLP is a limited liability partnership — a firm — not a company. This isn't a stylistic preference so much as it being the accurate legal structure of the business; calling it "the company" is simply incorrect. |
| **Engagement** | Contract | When referring to the client relationship itself (rather than a specific document), "engagement" is the term of art — it's what an "engagement letter" formalizes, and it covers the relationship whether the work is legal representation or organizational consulting. "Contract" is narrower and more transactional-sounding, and undersells the advisory, ongoing nature of the relationship. |
| **A context-specific verb** — "Send for review," "Save draft," "Send invoice" | Submit | "Submit" is a generic, bureaucratic verb that doesn't tell the reader what's actually about to happen. Naming the real action — what gets sent, saved, or shared, and to whom — is both clearer and more consistent with a plain-language voice. See the before/after pattern below, in the spirit of how [Shopify's Polaris](https://polaris.shopify.com/) content guidelines pair concrete before/after phrasing with each component rather than stating the principle in the abstract. |

## Before / after: generic verbs

| Generic | Context-specific |
| --- | --- |
| Submit | Send for review |
| Submit | Send invoice |
| Submit | Save draft |
| Submit | Request signature |

Each of these is a different action with a different result for the client on the other end of
it — collapsing them all into "Submit" hides that difference from the person clicking the
button. If a genuinely generic action doesn't have a more specific verb (a rare case), "Submit"
is an acceptable fallback rather than something to avoid at all costs — but it should be the
exception, checked for a better option first, not the default.

## A note on scope

Cedar Grove does two distinct kinds of work — legal representation and mission-driven
organizational consulting — and the terms above are meant to serve both without conflating
them. "Matter" is recommended as the umbrella term for a piece of client work specifically
*because* it's broad enough to cover both a litigation matter and a consulting engagement
without misdescribing either one, which is exactly the gap "case" (too litigation-specific) and
"project" (too generic) each leave open.

## How to use this page

- If a term below conflicts with how someone who actually works at the firm describes their
  own work, that's a signal to fix this page, not a reason to keep using the wrong word quietly.
- If a new domain term comes up that isn't covered here — a consulting-specific term, a new
  product feature, an internal shorthand that's starting to leak into client-facing copy —
  add it, following the same review path as any other change to this system (see
  **Governance**). A glossary that only grows when someone remembers to update it stops being
  reliable; the goal is to catch inconsistent usage while it's still one document, not after
  it's spread across a dozen screens.
