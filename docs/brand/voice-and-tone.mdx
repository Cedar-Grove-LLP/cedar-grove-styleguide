# Voice & Tone

## Start with what's real

The only piece of Cedar Grove copy that exists in production today is the tagline on the
live site: **"We help good people build good organizations."** Everything below is a
proposal for how to extend that one sentence into a full voice — not a transcription of
an existing style guide, because there isn't one yet. The current site is intentionally
minimal: a tagline, a nav (Mission / Practice / Team / Contact), and the logo. There's no
larger body of marketing copy to mine, and it's more useful to say that plainly than to
imply this page was reverse-engineered from a deep well of existing writing.

That constraint is actually a gift. It means the tagline gets to do more work than a
single sentence normally would — it's the clearest signal available for how Cedar Grove
wants to sound: warm without being casual, values-forward without being vague, and
credible on the legal side without reading like a document generator. Everything that
follows is built to sit comfortably next to that sentence, not to compete with it. Treat
this page as a strong starting recommendation the firm should push on, adjust, or
override — not a voice that's already locked in.

## Voice: who Cedar Grove always is

Voice doesn't change depending on where the words show up — that's tone's job, covered
further down. Voice is the constant personality underneath a marketing page, a billing
email, and an error message alike. Shopify's Polaris system is the model followed here:
name a small set of attributes, give each one a one-line definition, and show a real
do/don't pair rather than describing the attribute in the abstract. Four attributes are
proposed, chosen because the firm's work spans two registers — legal counsel and
mission-driven organizational consulting — and each attribute has to hold up in both.

| Attribute | What it means | Do | Don't |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Clear** | Plain language over legal jargon by default. Reserve a technical term for when it's actually load-bearing — "terminate the agreement" can just be "end the agreement" unless the legal distinction matters. | "We'll end the agreement on the date in your notice." | "The undersigned parties shall proceed to terminate the aforementioned agreement effective as of the date specified herein." |
| **Precise** | Say exactly what will happen and by when. Credibility rests on never being vague about consequences, especially the ones that cost a client time or money. | "If we don't hear back by June 30, we'll close your matter — reopening it will need a new engagement letter." | "Please get back to us soon, or there could be some changes to your matter status." |
| **Human** | Contractions are fine. "You" and "we" are fine. Match the warmth of "good people building good organizations" rather than sounding like an automated system. | "We noticed your hearing date moved — here's what changes for you." | "Notification: a modification to the previously scheduled hearing date has been recorded in the system." |
| **Composed** | Even urgent or difficult news is delivered calmly and directly. Never alarmist, never buried under hedging. | "The court moved your hearing to August 4. Here's what we need from you by July 28." | "URGENT!! Your hearing date has changed — please respond immediately!!" |

**An alternative worth naming:** a more traditional firm might add a fifth attribute like
*Authoritative* — leaning harder into formality as a signal of gravitas. That's a real,
defensible choice for a firm doing only conventional legal work. It's not recommended
here because it pulls in the wrong direction for a practice that's half mission-driven
organizational consulting, and it would sit awkwardly next to a tagline about "good
people" rather than "trusted counsel" or "decades of experience." *Clear* and *Precise*
already carry the credibility that *Authoritative* would be trying to add, without the
cost of sounding distant. If the firm's read on this is different, that's a legitimate
reason to reopen the list.

## Tone: how voice flexes by context

Voice is the constant; tone is the situational adjustment on top of it. GOV.UK's content
guidance draws this line clearly and it's worth borrowing directly: the same voice
handles renewing a passport and reporting a death, but the tone in each case is obviously
not the same weight. Below are four contexts where Cedar Grove's tone should shift, with
the same four voice attributes underneath every one of them.

### Firm / marketing site

Confident, warm, mission-forward — matching the energy of the real tagline. Still plain
language, not startup-cheerful; Cedar Grove is not selling a SaaS product.

- **Do:** "We help good people build good organizations. That's the whole practice —
  legal counsel and organizational strategy for founders who started something because
  they believed in it."
- **Don't:** "🚀 We're revolutionizing legal services for changemakers like you!"

### In-product transactional UI

Direct, efficient, minimal. The user is trying to get something done, not read prose.

- **Do:** "Matter created."
- **Don't:** "Woohoo! Your matter is ready to go!"

### Error / problem states

Calm, specific, and never a hint of blaming the person reading it — the error is the
system's problem to explain, not the user's fault to own.

- **Do:** "We couldn't save this document. Check your connection and try again."
- **Don't:** "You didn't enter this correctly." *(blames the user)* or "Something went
  wrong." *(too vague to act on)*

### Formal / compliance content

Engagement letters, billing summaries, anything with legal weight. Precise and neutral —
and this is the one context where it's worth explicitly separating two things that look
similar but aren't:

- **Required legal precision** — defined terms of art that carry real legal meaning and
  should stay exactly as-is: *attorney-client privilege*, *conflict of interest*,
  *engagement letter*. Simplifying these doesn't make them clearer, it makes them wrong.
- **Habitual legal jargon** — phrasing that's just inherited convention with no legal
  weight of its own: *pursuant to*, *hereinafter*, *the party of the first part*. These
  should default to plain language unless a specific drafting reason says otherwise.

- **Do:** "This letter confirms our engagement to represent you in the matter described
  below. Our fees are billed monthly, in the amounts set out in Schedule A."
- **Don't:** "Pursuant to the terms hereinafter set forth, the undersigned law firm
  (hereinafter 'Counsel') shall render legal services to the party of the first part."

## This isn't final

Everything on this page — the four attributes, the four contexts, the examples — is a
recommendation built to extend the one real sentence Cedar Grove has published, not a
voice the firm has signed off on. Treat it as the strongest starting proposal available
today, and change what doesn't fit once real people at the firm weigh in.
